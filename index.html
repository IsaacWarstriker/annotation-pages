The unit

One mile of road, fully annotated — lanes, connections, and the traffic rules bound to them. Counted per period: 20 working days, roughly one month. Days spent encoding rules (signs, lights, right-of-way) produce zero miles and are part of the work; the realization rate accounts for them.

Unit guard. Annotated lanelet miles are not route miles. One route mile contains several lanelet miles — lanes × directions × ramps. Any client figure expressed in route or corridor miles is converted with a per-corridor factor before a commitment is made.
A two-lane-per-direction road: one route mile ≈ four annotated lanelet miles. The factor is measured per corridor.
The annotation, one layer up: lanelet segments plus the regulatory elements — signs, lights, stop lines — bound to the exact lanes they govern. Rule-encoding days produce these bindings, not mileage.

The ramp

A new annotator produces 5 miles in the first period and adds 5 per month — full speed of 30 miles per period at Month 6. Targets assume hard terrain; easier areas outperform the plan rather than raising it. High performers show ~36; planning always uses the floor.

Waves and teams

A team: 1 lead + 4 annotators.
lead promoted from within

Leads are promoted from within — annotators who did the miles. They own first-line quality, which is what lets the division scale.

A wave: 20 hires = 4 future teams.

Wave size matches training and supervision capacity. Larger waves add headcount faster than they add reliable output.

The Month-6 step is smaller than the ramp promises — by design. At each wave's month 6, four producers become leads (16 annotators + 4 leads at ~70% output), so the wave lands at 564, not 600. That gap is the visible price of a permanent quality and training layer; there is no dip, just a shallower final step.

Reading 500 and 1000

The figures answer two different questions. Both are on page 1 — the view toggle switches between them.

As a run rate (miles per period, sustained)

A single wave sustains ≈450 from Month 6 (lead extraction lands at M6). 500+/period sustained requires two waves — 40 people (≈690 exiting Month 6, ≈900 at Month 9). 1000/period requires a third wave: ≈1,140 committed at Month 12, decided at the Month-9 review.

As a delivered volume (cumulative miles)

A single wave crosses 500 committed miles at the start of Month 4 and 1000 in mid-Month 5 (≈1,680 banked by the end of Month 6). Volume asks are usually this question, not the run-rate one.

The evidence system

Nobody advances on time served or speed alone. Every 20-day period closes at a certification gate that writes a permanent evidence record — evaluation, output metrics, and the lead's continuous log. At Month 5, lead candidates are flagged from that record, not from opinion; teams form at Month 6 with a paper trail behind every promotion. The numbers on this page assume that machinery — so we built it.

Design questions

The best questions we received didn't get answers — they got built in. Each one below reshaped the model before it reached this page.

Why commit below what the model shows?

A commitment has to survive a hard-terrain month or a heavy rework cycle. Committing at 80% pre-pays normal friction: the division absorbs bad weeks and outperforms in good ones. Committed numbers are floors.

Where does lead extraction show in the numbers?

At each wave’s month 6, four producers step up as leads (16 + 4 at ~70% output), so the wave’s full-speed month lands at 564 instead of 600. The strongest producers trade short-term miles for a permanent quality and training layer — the cost is a shallower step, not a drop.

Could the work be split — new hires draw lanes, veterans add signs?

The map format encodes rules as relationships: a sign is meaningless until bound to the exact lanes it governs. Splitting by element forces the second person to re-learn the area and concentrates errors in the most client-visible category. One annotator owns one area end to end; work is split by phase, not by person.

Why waves of 20 rather than 40?

Annotation scales with hiring. Review does not — every client-ready mile passes through a lead's final review, and that gate is fixed per lead. Today the gate is even narrower: three platform-experienced coordinators carry training, calibration and review for everyone. Doubling the wave doubles the intake without widening the gate; the surplus becomes backlog, not delivery. A second wave starts the moment demand justifies it — that is the faster path that actually delivers.

What one corridor mile really costs

This is the unit guard with measured numbers. A sample extent — a 10-mile corridor, road data from public OSM — was run through the workload predictor: 78.9 centerline miles, 137.6 lanelet (lane) miles, 616 segments. Annotation expansion is always reported as a multiplier, never a percentage, and always in its two stages — because they behave differently:

The 7.9× is this extent's road density — a rural corridor will not repeat it. The 1.74× is physics — lanes per road — and travels between jobs. "13.8×" quoted alone invites the wrong mental model.

Productivity — one factor per cause

Per-head steady output already includes lead extraction (leads at 70% partial output): 28.2 lane-miles / head / month at 100% — the same number the capacity model produces, which is the internal-consistency check. Two adjustments apply, each counted once: the complexity rate carries road difficulty, and realization stays at 80 carrying only operational overhead — rule-encoding days, QC and rework, meetings, ramp variance. Road mix never flows through realization; nothing gets banked twice.

The tier system behind the rates. Corridors are scored additively (2–10 points: length, intersection density, access spacing, topology) by a scoring tool that runs in parallel with the workload predictor. Four tiers fall out of the score — the profiles are centroids, not definitions: Easy (score 2–3, ×1.6) — highways and open roads, minimal lane complexity; Regular (4–5, ×1.3) — suburban or rural, some merges and geometry changes, low editing overhead; Complex (6–7, ×1.0) — urban and semi-urban, intersection-dense, turn lanes, approaching interchanges; Super-complex (8–10, ×0.5) — dense multi-level corridors, distribuidores, stacked ramps and bridges, continuous access.

The planning model collapses this to three buckets:

Default: 20 / 40 / 40 → ×1.02, effectively neutral. The two Regular sliders complement each other inside the remaining 80. Easy tier (×1.6) exists in the rubric as upside; it is not planned into the mix. Chain: soft basis × complexity rate × 80% realization → committed.

Why the Complex 20% is locked. The ×0.5 bucket is the volatility driver of any estimate — it halves throughput wherever it appears, and it hides in places that look small on a map: parking lots, stacked-geometry overpasses, multi-level interchanges. Locking its planning share at 20% bounds the downside a schedule can silently absorb. A corridor mix scoring above 20% in this bucket is not planned inside the model — it becomes a named exception, scoped per-AOI with its own predictor run and its own dates.
